# 134: History in the tooltips: the research pass

The research pass for [issue 134](../issues/134-history-in-the-tooltips.md) (from [131](../issues/131-v1.md), decision 8). For each control with a musical past, it gathers where the control comes from, with a source a reader can follow and a note on how sure each fact is. Each section ends with a **draft History line** in the tips' voice. The drafts are raw material for the owner's grilling, not final words. Claude's research pass, 2026-10-31, open to the owner.

## How to read it

Each fact carries a confidence mark:

- **A**: read in a primary source: a patent, the maker's manual or statement, the inventor's paper, a museum record. Read for this pass, quoted or paraphrased closely.
- **B**: primary, but only partly read, or a maker's own claim of a "first", which nobody independent has checked.
- **C**: secondary only (an encyclopaedia, a magazine, a book cited by one and not read here). Fine as background; a History line resting on a C needs the primary found first, or softer words.
- **U**: not sourced. Kept so the grilling knows the gap is there.

The scanned manuals and the Moog paper were read by optical character recognition, page by page; "scan n" and "PDF page n" refer to the scan's own page order, not the printed page numbers.

## The Filter and its controls

### Filter (the Section)

- Robert Moog's filter patent: four identical transistor stages in a ladder; filed 1966, granted 1969. **A** [S1]
- Moog's 1964 paper already built voltage-controlled low-pass, high-pass and band-pass filters from his variable-gain amplifier (PDF pages 7–8). **A** [S2]
- The Minimoog's sound modifiers: "a lowpass filter and an amplifier, both of which have their own contour generators" (scan 3). **A** [S3]
- The ARP 2600's filter (its manual, 1971): low-pass, "variable cutoff frequency (Fc) and resonance (Q)", falling "at 24Db per octave" (PDF page 36). **A** [S5]

**Draft History:** Robert Moog patented his ladder Filter in 1969 (filed 1966): four transistor stages in a row. The Minimoog (1970) carried it, and it became the sound of subtractive synthesis.

*Note:* "the sound of subtractive synthesis" is a judgement, not a sourced fact; the grilling may prefer to cut it. That the Minimoog's filter is the ladder is **C**: its manual says only "a lowpass filter".

### Cutoff

- The ARP 2600 manual names it "cutoff frequency (Fc)", 10 Hz to 10 kHz by hand, 1 Hz to 20 kHz under voltage control (PDF page 36). **A** [S5]
- The Minimoog's control is CUTOFF FREQUENCY (scan 12). **A** [S3]

**Draft History:** Moog's 1964 paper set a filter's frequency by a voltage, so a keyboard or an Envelope could move it; the Minimoog's knob was already called Cutoff Frequency.

### Resonance

- Moog's patent: an adjustable feedback resistor from output to input makes the low-pass resonant at the cutoff; enough feedback makes the filter oscillate, "a phase shift oscillator with four voltage-variable phase-shifting sections". **A** [S1]
- The Minimoog calls it EMPHASIS; at 10 the filter "breaks into oscillation, and produces a pure sine wave tone", a "sixth sound source" (scans 14–15). **A** [S3]
- ARP calls the same thing resonance, written Q (PDF page 36). **A** [S5]
- For the Synth Geek Layer: the letter Q was first used by K. S. Johnson of Western Electric (book of 1924); "quality factor" was coined by Legg and Given at Bell Labs in 1940. **C** [S23]

**Draft History:** In Moog's patent, Resonance is feedback from the Filter's output to its input; turned up far enough, the Filter rings by itself. The Minimoog called it Emphasis.

### Filter Mode

- Moog, 1964: low-pass, high-pass and band-pass filters, each from a variable-gain amplifier (PDF pages 7–8). **A** [S2]
- The Oberheim SEM (1974): a state-variable filter giving low-pass, high-pass and band-pass at 12 dB an octave, with a notch from low-pass and high-pass summed. **C** [S28]

**Draft History:** One Filter with four Modes, low, high, band and notch, is the state-variable Filter of Tom Oberheim's SEM (1974).

*Confidence:* C. The SEM's year and its notch rest on reviews and on a maker of modern copies; the Oberheim manual was not found.

### Slope

- Moog's ladder has four stages ("four RC sections"); the patent gives no dB figure. Four sections fall at 24 dB an octave: Claude's inference, not the patent's words. **A** (stages) / inference (slope) [S1]
- ARP 2600, 1971: "24Db per octave". **A** [S5]
- Oberheim SEM: 12 dB an octave. **C** [S28]

**Draft History:** 24 dB is Moog's four-stage ladder; 12 dB is the gentler two-stage Filter of Oberheim's SEM.

### Key Track

- The Minimoog's two KEYBOARD CONTROL switches move the cutoff with the key played: without them "the higher notes on the keyboard will sound duller than the lower ones"; with both on, the cutoff "moves in full response" to the keyboard (scan 15). **A** [S3]

**Draft History:** The Minimoog had it as two Keyboard Control switches, a little and a lot: both on, the Filter followed the keys in full, so high Notes stayed bright.

### Velocity → Cutoff

- Moog, 1964: a hand-capacitance sensor, differentiated, gives a gain "proportional to the velocity of the musician's hand"; "velocity sensors produce percussive amplitude envelopes" (PDF page 10). **A** [S2]
- The Yamaha CS-80 (1977): a velocity- and pressure-sensitive keyboard, velocity able to move the filter cutoff. **C** [S29]
- MIDI (1983) sends a velocity with every Note. **U** (MIDI 1.0 specification not read).

**Draft History:** Moog was measuring how fast a hand struck in 1964; Yamaha's CS-80 (1977) let a hard key open the Filter, and MIDI (1983) sends that speed with every Note.

*A-only alternative:* "Moog was already sensing how fast a hand struck in 1964, to give each Note a percussive start."

### Envelope Amount

- The Minimoog's AMOUNT OF CONTOUR "determines the amount of filter contour applied to the filter's cutoff frequency" (scan 12). **A** [S3]

**Draft History:** The Minimoog's knob for this was Amount of Contour: how far its filter Envelope swept the Cutoff.

## The Envelopes

### Amp Envelope, Filter Envelope, and Attack, Decay, Sustain, Release

- Buchla 100 Series, 1964: the Model 180 Dual Attack Generator, "envelope control voltages initiated by pulses", with attack (.002 to 1 s), duration and decay (.002 to 5 s). **B** [S7] (a period module list reprinted in 1992; its "1964" is the list's heading).
- Moog, after talks with Vladimir Ussachevsky of the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center in 1965, built an envelope module with T1 (attack time), T2 (initial decay), Esus (sustain level) and T3 (final decay). **C** [S30] citing [S8] p. 59, not read.
- The Minimoog's contour generators: ATTACK TIME, DECAY TIME, SUSTAIN LEVEL, and a DECAY switch that makes the sound fade on letting go "rather than immediately upon release of a key" (scans 13–16). **A** [S3]
- The ARP 2600 manual, 1971, labels its generator "ADSR / Attack-Decay-Sustain-Release ENV GEN", beside a simpler AR (attack–release) one (PDF pages 74–75). **A** [S5] This is the earliest printed "ADSR" found in this pass, not shown to be the first.

**Draft History (Amp Envelope or the stages):** Buchla's 1964 attack generator had an attack, a duration and a decay; Moog's took a sustain level at Ussachevsky's suggestion; ARP's 2600 manual (1971) named the four stages Attack, Decay, Sustain, Release.

**Draft History (Filter Envelope):** The Minimoog gave its Filter an Envelope of its own beside the loudness one (1970): the same shape, moving brightness instead of level.

*Confidence:* the Ussachevsky half is C until *Analog Days* p. 59 (or a Moog document) is read.

## The Oscillators

### Oscillators (the Section)

- Moog, 1964: an oscillator whose frequency follows a control current, giving sawtooth, triangle and a pulse (PDF pages 4–5); "the systematic use of voltage control" is the paper's main point (PDF page 3). **A** [S2]
- The Minimoog: three oscillators, one noise source and a microphone preamplifier, "five sound sources" (scan 3). **A** [S3]

**Draft History:** Robert Moog described the voltage-controlled oscillator to the Audio Engineering Society in October 1964: a pitch set by a voltage, so a keyboard, a knob or another oscillator can play it.

### Waveform

- Moog, 1964: sawtooth, triangle ("the characteristic mellow, flute-like timbre of the triangular wave") and a pulse (PDF page 5). **A** [S2]
- The Minimoog: triangular, sawtooth-triangular, sawtooth, square, wide and narrow rectangular; "The triangular waveform has the least harmonic content; the narrow rectangular has the most" (scans 6–7). **A** [S3]
- The ARP 2600: sine, square, sawtooth, triangle and variable-width pulse (PDF page 27). **A** [S5]
- Behind the Harmonics: Fourier (1822) showed a repeating wave as a sum of sines; Helmholtz (1863) heard timbre as the mix of a tone's harmonics. **B** (bibliographic records checked; the passages not read) [S25], [S26]

**Draft History:** Moog's first oscillator (1964) gave a saw, a triangle and a pulse; the sum-of-sines that explains them is Fourier's (1822), and Helmholtz (1863) explained timbre as that mix.

### Pulse Width

- Moog, 1964: "The PULSE WIDTH control … varies the pulse width from 10% to 50% of the period" (PDF page 5). **A** [S2]
- The Prophet-5's Poly Mod can move Osc A's pulse width (the square wave). **A** [S16]

**Draft History:** Moog's 1964 oscillator already had a Pulse Width knob, from 10 % to 50 %. Moving it with an LFO is PWM.

### Octave (Osc 1 and Osc 2)

- The Minimoog's RANGE switch: six positions, all but LO "separated by one octave", marked in feet (32′, 16′, 8′…), the LO position "sub-audio" (scan 6). **A** [S3]
- That the feet come from organ pipes (an 8′ pipe sounds the written pitch) is **U** here: well known, but no source was read.

**Draft History:** The Minimoog marked its octaves in feet, 32′ to 2′, as an organ marks its stops: 8′ is the Note as written.

### Semitone and Tuning

- The Minimoog's Osc 2 and Osc 3 FREQUENCY knobs move them "as much as a major sixth" from Osc 1; "turn control (7) … until a perfect fifth is made. Once again, this interval will remain constant" (scan 7). **A** [S3]

**Draft History:** The Minimoog tuned its second and third oscillators by ear, up to a sixth from the first: a fifth held across the keyboard is two Notes from one key.

### Detune

- Pipe organs: a celeste rank, tuned a little off a normal rank, beats against it, typically 0.5 to 5 times a second; Hope-Jones built three-rank celestes, as at Worcester Cathedral in 1896. **C** [S24]
- The Minimoog's manual tunes Osc 2 "nearly in unison" with Osc 1 (scan 7). **A** [S3]
- ARP's 3620 keyboard (1976): "multiple vibratos for chorus effects". **A** [S6]

**Draft History:** Organ builders did it with pipes by the 1890s: a celeste rank, tuned a hair off its partner, beats slowly against it.

*Confidence:* C. The celeste's first appearance was not found; Britannica's entry (19th-century Romantic organ) refused the fetch.

### Env Amount (Osc 2)

- The Prophet-5's Poly Mod: the Filter Envelope as a source, Oscillator A's frequency as a destination; the manual's "synth brass" uses it for a slight rise and fall of pitch at each Note. **A** [S16]
- The Prophet-5 debuted at Winter NAMM in 1978. **B** [S17]

**Draft History:** Sequential's Prophet-5 (1978) did it with Poly Mod: the Filter Envelope bending an oscillator's pitch, for a brass Note's slight rise and fall.

### FM

- John Chowning, *J. Audio Eng. Soc.*, September 1973: "a surprising control of audio spectra" from frequency modulation at audio rate. **A** (record and abstract) [S13]
- Stanford's patent, filed 1975, granted 1977. **A** [S14] Stanford and Yamaha signed an FM licence on 19 March 1975. **A** (the archive's dated list) [S15]
- The Yamaha DX7 (1983) took FM to the mass market. **C** [S31]
- Analogue cross-modulation, as Limpide does it: the Prophet-5's Poly Mod sends Osc B to Osc A's frequency (destinations listed, [S16], **A**); the Minimoog's Osc 3 could modulate the other oscillators' pitch through the Modulation Mix (scan 8, [S3], **A**).

**Draft History:** John Chowning found in 1973, at Stanford, that one sine swinging another at audio rate makes whole families of partials; Yamaha licensed it in 1975 and built the DX7 (1983) on it.

*Note for the grilling:* Limpide's FM is analogue-style linear FM (cross-modulation), the Prophet-5's Poly Mod kind, while Chowning's is digital (the paper's formula is in fact phase modulation, per a patent commentary seen only in a search summary: **C**). A line naming the Prophet-5 instead would be more exact for what Limpide does.

### Ring

- Clyde Keith's Bell Labs patent: a double-balanced modulator of four rectifiers, "typically copper oxide", filed 1929, granted 1932. **A** [S9]
- Frank Cowan's AT&T patent: four rectifiers "in a closed loop", the carrier suppressed; filed 1934, granted 1935. **A** [S10]
- Both were for telephony (frequency translation for carrier systems). **A** [S9], [S10]
- Buchla 100 Series, 1964: the Model 111 Dual Ring Modulator, "the sums and differences between frequency components of two input signals", the originals "suppressed about 55 db". **B** [S7]
- Moog, 1964: "ring modulators" among the specialised modules that work with his (PDF page 10). **A** [S2]
- The ARP 2600: "essentially a voltage multiplier … the sum and difference frequencies" (PDF page 45). **A** [S5]
- Stockhausen's *Mixtur*, for orchestra, sine-wave generators and ring modulators: **A** (instrumentation) [S11]; 1964: **C** [S32].
- Harald Bode's ring modulators (Melochord, 1947; Moog licensed his designs in the 1960s); the Daleks' voices on *Doctor Who* (1963). **C** [S12]; dates differ between secondary sources (1963–64 or 1966 for the Moog licence).

**Draft History:** Ring Modulation began in the telephone network: a ring of four rectifiers, filed by AT&T's Frank Cowan in 1934 after Clyde Keith's Bell Labs design of 1929. Stockhausen's *Mixtur* (1964) sent an orchestra through four of them.

*Alternative, if the Daleks are wanted:* "…and in 1963 it gave the Daleks their voices." Needs a primary source (a BBC Radiophonic Workshop account) first.

### Sub

- The Roland SH-101 (introduced 1982): a sub-oscillator, 1 octave down square, 2 octaves down square or 2 octaves down pulse. **A** [S20]
- Earlier Roland monosynths (the SH-1, the SH-3 of 1974) had one too. **C** (vintage-gear sites only).
- Who put the first sub-oscillator on a synth: **U**.

**Draft History:** Roland's SH-101 (1982) had one beside its single oscillator: a square one or two octaves down, or a narrow pulse two down.

*Not used:* that such a sub is made by halving the oscillator's frequency (a divider) is how they are usually built, but no source was read for the SH-101's circuit: **U**.

### Noise and Noise colour

- J. B. Johnson measured the thermal hiss of resistors at Bell Labs, *Phys. Rev.*, 1928 (white noise's physical source). **C** [S22]
- Johnson's 1925 paper found a low-frequency noise rising as frequency falls, which Schottky named the "flicker effect" (the 1/f, pink kind). **C** [S22]
- Buchla 100 Series, 1964: Model 160 White Noise Generator. **B** [S7]
- The Minimoog: "Two 'colors' of noise are available—white, or high-pitched, and pink or low-pitched", on a WHITE/PINK switch (scans 11–12). **A** [S3]

**Draft History:** The Minimoog already had both colours on one switch, white "high-pitched" and pink "low-pitched", in its own words.

### Mixer

- The Minimoog: the three oscillators, the noise source and a microphone preamplifier "are combined and balanced by the Mixer" (scan 11). **A** [S3]

**Draft History:** The Minimoog put a Mixer between its sources and its Filter, as here: three oscillators, noise, and a microphone input.

## The Amp

### Amp (the Section) and Volume

- Moog, 1964: the "variable gain amplifier", its gain set by a control voltage (PDF page 6). **A** [S2]

**Draft History:** Moog's 1964 paper described the voltage-controlled amplifier: loudness set by a voltage, so an Envelope can shape each Note.

### Voices

- The Minimoog: "If more than one key is held down, only the lowest one has effect" (scan 10). **A** [S3]
- The Prophet-5: five voices, "the world's first fully-programmable polyphonic synthesizer", debuted at Winter NAMM 1978. **B** (the maker's claim) [S17]; Dave Smith: "I was 28 when I created the original Prophet-5" [S16].

**Draft History:** The Minimoog (1970) played one Note, the lowest key held; Sequential's Prophet-5 (1978) played five, and stored every knob in memory.

*Note:* Limpide's 1 Voice plays the newest key, not the lowest; the line must not suggest otherwise.

## The LFOs

### LFO, Rate and Depth

- Moog, 1964: the oscillator's lowest range, ".02-5 cps", "is useful in timing and modulation applications" (PDF page 5). **A** [S2]
- The Minimoog had no separate LFO: Oscillator 3 set to LO "oscillates only a few times a second"; the MODULATION wheel "is in effect a level control" for it (scan 8). **A** [S3]
- The ARP 2600, 1971: each oscillator has an LF range from .03 Hz, "one cycle approximately every 30 seconds"; "a much smoother and more flexible vibrato is created by using a low-frequency sine wave" (PDF pages 21, 34). **A** [S5]
- ARP's 3620 keyboard (1976): "A built-in low frequency oscillator (LFO) gives you an expressive vibrato". **A** [S6] The earliest printed "LFO" found here; not shown to be the first.

**Draft History:** Early synths had no LFO of their own: the Minimoog (1970) slowed its third oscillator below hearing, and the mod wheel set how much it moved the pitch. ARP's manuals called it a low frequency oscillator, LFO, by 1976.

### Retrigger

- Where key sync of an LFO first appeared: **U**. (ARP's 3620 has a Trigger Mode switch, but it governs the keyboard's triggers, not the LFO: [S6].)

**Draft History:** none proposed until a source is found.

## Playing

### Glide

- Theremin's patent: pitch changed continuously by a hand's distance, "continuous variations … with most delicate graduations"; filed 1925, granted 1928. **A** [S21] Moog dates the theremin to 1920 and names the Trautonium and the Ondes Martenot as the prewar instruments that let a player bend pitch (PDF page 3). **A** [S2]
- Moog, 1964: his ribbon controller "varied continuously to produce glissandos, vibratos" (PDF page 9). **A** [S2]
- The Minimoog: "The GLIDE control … regulates the amount of portamento, or glide, heard between pitches" (scan 10). **A** [S3]
- The ARP 2600's keyboard had portamento (PDF page 27). **A** [S5]

**Draft History:** Electronic instruments slid between pitches from the theremin (1920) on; the Minimoog (1970) put it on a knob and called it Glide.

### Pitch bend range, the mod wheel and the pedal

- The Minimoog's PITCH wheel bends "as much as half an octave up or down", with "a detent mechanism" holding its centre; the MODULATION wheel beside it (scans 8, 10). **A** [S3]
- The Minimoog as the first synth with a pitch wheel: **C** (Wikipedia, citing a Yamaha article not read).
- A bend range of 2 semitones as the MIDI default (General MIDI, RPN 0): **C** (the specification not read).
- The Minimoog's S-TRIG plug "keeps the contour generators 'on' continuously. It can be wired to an accessory foot switch" (scan 18). **A** [S3]

**Draft History:** The Minimoog (1970) had both wheels left of its keys: its pitch wheel bent half an octave each way and clicked back to the middle.

### Hold

- See the Minimoog's S-TRIG foot switch above: a plug that keeps the Envelopes "on continuously". **A** [S3]

**Draft History:** The Minimoog did it with a foot switch wired to a plug: while pressed, the Envelopes stayed open as if the key were still down.

*Note:* thin. The piano's damper pedal is the older ancestor, but no source was read: **U**.

### MIDI (MIDI panel, MIDI Learn, Quick Setup)

- Dave Smith of Sequential presented the Universal Synthesizer Interface to the AES in 1981. **B** [S19]
- At Winter NAMM, January 1983, a Sequential Prophet 600 played a Roland Jupiter 6 over MIDI; the Prophet 600 was the first MIDI synth. **B** [S18], [S19]
- Dave Smith and Roland's Ikutaro Kakehashi received a Technical Grammy for MIDI (2013). **B** [S18]
- An early MIDI 1.0 specification is dated August 1983. **B** [S19]

**Draft History:** MIDI was first played in public in January 1983: a Sequential Prophet 600 driving a Roland Jupiter 6. Dave Smith and Ikutaro Kakehashi shared a Grammy for it in 2013.

## What the grilling should settle

1. **Which tips get a line:** the issue's shortlist (the Filter, the Envelopes, the LFO, Ring, FM, the Sub, Glide), or every control above with an A-sourced line (about 25), or the Sections alone.
2. **The bar for a fact:** A only, or B allowed (a maker's own "first"), and whether C facts are dropped or sent back for their primary source (Ussachevsky, the SEM, the Daleks, the DX7).
3. **"First" claims:** banned, or allowed only with the maker's name on them ("Sequential calls it the first…"). Most drafts above avoid the word.
4. **Instrument names and their labels:** may a line quote another instrument's own words (Emphasis, Amount of Contour, Poly Mod, contour) beside the glossary's, and name products (Minimoog, Prophet-5, DX7, SH-101)?
5. **People:** name the people (Moog, Buchla, Ussachevsky, Chowning, Smith, Kakehashi, Oberheim, Stockhausen) or only the instruments?
6. **FM's line:** Chowning's digital FM (famous) or the Prophet-5's analogue Poly Mod (what Limpide actually does)?
7. **Length and phone:** one sentence of about 120 characters, or up to two (most drafts run 150–200); and whether the line folds on a phone.
8. **The Viewers:** do the Scope, the Spectrum and the Harmonic Markers get History (Fourier, Helmholtz, Cooley–Tukey), or only the instrument's controls?
9. **Where the sources live:** this file as the source list, linked from the About, or a shorter page made for readers; and whether the fact-checker reads this file before a release.
10. **Shared tips:** Attack, Decay, Sustain and Release share one tip for both Envelopes; one History line for all four, or one per stage?
